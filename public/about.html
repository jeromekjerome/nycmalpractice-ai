New York's Medical Malpractice Framework

Medical malpractice in New York occurs when a healthcare provider deviates from the accepted standard of care and that deviation causes injury. The legal framework includes several key rules:

  • Standard of care: What a reasonably competent provider in the same specialty would have done under the same circumstances
  • CPLR §214-a: The 2.5-year statute of limitations for personal injury malpractice claims
  • CPLR §3012-a (Certificate of Merit): An attorney must certify that a qualified expert has reviewed the case before or at the time of filing
  • CPLR Article 16: Limits non-economic damage liability for defendants found less than 50% responsible
  • Continuous treatment doctrine: The SOL is tolled (paused) during continuous treatment by the same provider for the same condition
  • Foreign object exception: No statute of limitations when a foreign object is negligently left in the body
  • Infancy toll: The SOL does not run against a minor patient

The Wrongful Death Distinction

When a patient dies due to malpractice, two distinct claims may exist: a survival action (for conscious pain and suffering before death, brought by the estate) and a wrongful death action (for the pecuniary loss suffered by the deceased's distributees — typically lost earnings, lost services, and loss of parental guidance). The wrongful death SOL is 2 years from the date of death under EPTL §5-4.1 — a shorter window than the standard malpractice SOL.

⚠️ The wrongful death statute of limitations is only 2 years from the date of death. If your loved one passed away due to suspected malpractice, consult an attorney immediately.

Why Expert Witnesses Are Essential

Unlike most personal injury cases, medical malpractice claims require expert medical testimony to establish (1) what the applicable standard of care was, (2) how the defendant deviated from it, and (3) how that deviation caused the injury. Malpractice attorneys typically have established relationships with medical experts in the relevant specialty — which is a primary reason to work with a specialist rather than a general litigator.
